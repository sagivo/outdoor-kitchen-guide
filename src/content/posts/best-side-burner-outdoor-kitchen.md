A side burner is the most underrated appliance in an outdoor kitchen. Your grill head does one thing brilliantly — sear and cook over direct flame — but the moment you need to simmer a sauce, boil corn, or sauté vegetables while the steaks are on, you're running back to the indoor kitchen. A built-in side burner fixes that. It gives you a real stovetop burner, plumbed into the same gas supply as your grill, right in the island. Here are the best options for 2026, with real specs and sizing guidance so you buy the right unit the first time.

## What a side burner actually does for you

Think of the side burner as the stovetop half of your outdoor kitchen. With it, you can:

- **Simmer sauces and gravies** while the grill is loaded with meat.
- **Boil corn, potatoes, and pasta** outside instead of steaming up the house.
- **Sauté vegetables** in a skillet as a side course.
- **Keep sauces warm** on low while you rest the steaks.

Without one, every side dish is a trip indoors — and in the middle of a cookout, that breaks your flow. Builders call the side burner the "second station," and it's one of the most-used components in a well-planned island.

## What to look for

**BTU output.** A single burner at 10,000–12,000 BTU behaves like a medium-high burner on your indoor range — ideal for sauces, sautés, and boiling a pot of corn. Dual burners in the 22,000–30,000 BTU range give you two heat zones or one big-pot station. Power burners at 60,000 BTU are a different animal: built for seafood boils, stockpots, and wok cooking at volume. Buy the BTU for what you'll actually cook, not for bragging rights.

**Burner material.** Brass burners (Blaze, Bull) retain heat exceptionally well and resist corrosion. Stainless steel burners (Napoleon) are easy to clean and won't rust. Both are legitimate — the bigger durability question is the body of the unit, which should be heavy-gauge 304 stainless steel if it lives outdoors year-round.

**Cutout dimensions.** This is the spec that trips up buyers. Each side burner needs a specific cutout in the countertop, and they're not standardized across brands. Measure your island's available space, then check the manufacturer's cutout drawing before ordering — never assume two "12-inch" burners have the same cutout.

**Ignition.** Battery-free piezo ignition (Napoleon's JETFIRE system, for example) means no batteries to die in the weather. Push-to-turn ignition with flame-sensing is standard on Blaze and Bull. Whichever you get, confirm it has a thermocouple or flame-safety shutoff — a gust of wind shouldn't leave gas flowing.

**Fuel type.** Side burners come in propane and natural gas versions, and the fittings differ. If your island runs on [natural gas](/posts/natural-gas-vs-propane-grill/), buy the NG model; if you're feeding it from a tank, buy the propane version. Some units ship with conversion kits — check the listing.

**Cover.** A stainless steel cover is essential, not optional. An uncovered burner fills with rainwater, leaves, and grease between cooks. Every unit below ships with a cover.

## The best built-in side burners

### Blaze double side burner (BLZ-SB2LTE) — best all-around

Blaze's double side burner pairs two 12,000 BTU cast brass burners for 24,000 BTU total, in a 304 stainless steel body with a removable stainless lid. Cast brass burners are the standout feature: brass holds heat superbly, which means steadier simmering and faster recovery when you drop a cold pot on the flame. The LTE version adds LED control-panel lighting so you can cook after dark — a genuinely useful feature for anyone who entertains in the evening. Cutout is 10¾" wide × 20¾" deep, so it drops into a narrow island run without eating your prep space. If you're already building around a [Blaze grill](/grills/blaze-premium-lte-32/), this is the natural match.

### Blaze single side burner (BLZ-SB1) — best simple single burner

The same cast brass burner design in a single-burner unit at 12,000 BTU, with a stainless cover and push-and-turn ignition. This is the right pick if your island is tight on space and you mainly need a sauce and side station — simmering marinara, warming beans, boiling a pot of water while the grill does the heavy lifting. It drops into a compact cutout and costs noticeably less than dual-burner units, leaving budget for other island components.

### Napoleon 500 Series single range burner (BI10RTPSS-CE) — best budget brand match

Napoleon's drop-in single burner delivers 10,000 BTU across a 9" × 10.25" cooking area — roughly a 93-square-inch surface — in stainless steel with a stainless protective cover and battery-free JETFIRE ignition. It includes a seamless trim ring for a clean install and matches the Napoleon 500 Series grill line aesthetically. For a homeowner building a Napoleon island who needs a straightforward side station, this is the obvious, cost-effective choice.

### Napoleon 700 Series 12" inline dual range burner — best dual burner for 700 islands

Stepping up to the 700 Series, the 12-inch inline dual range burner offers about 30,000 BTU across roughly 200 square inches of cooking area — two burners in one drop-in footprint. It uses Napoleon's JETFIRE ignition and the brand's signature blue-to-red NIGHT LIGHT control knobs, which glow blue at rest and turn red when a burner is lit — a safety plus in low light. List pricing lands around the $850–$950 range. One installation note: if you're installing a 700 Series burner without a 700 Series grill alongside it, Napoleon requires the N370-1091-SER power supply kit for the lit knobs.

### Bull drop-in double side burner (30009) — best for Bull islands

Bull's drop-in double side burner runs two burners at 22,000 BTU total in 304 stainless steel, designed to sit flush in a Bull island system. Street pricing typically falls in the $700–$950 range depending on retailer and fuel type. Bull builds its island components as a matched system in Lodi, California — if your island is already Bull (the [Bull Outlaw 30](/grills/bull-outlaw-30/) is one of the most common built-in heads in this price class), the double side burner drops in with documented cutouts and consistent fit and finish.

### Napoleon 700 Series dual infrared burner (BIB18IRNSS) — best for searing

Not a traditional side burner at all — this 18-inch drop-in unit uses two infrared burners totaling 30,000 BTU across 310 square inches, Napoleon calls the zone its SIZZLE ZONE, and the marketing numbers claim temperatures up to 1,800°F for steakhouse-style sears. As an infrared unit it behaves differently from a conventional burner: intense radiant heat, less convection, phenomenal crust. It's the right pick if your island's weak spot is searing, not saucing — think of it as a second, sear-focused cooking station rather than a stovetop. It does not replace a conventional side burner for simmering.

### Napoleon 700 Series power burner (BIB18) — best for seafood boils

The heavyweight option: 60,000 BTU across 310 square inches, built for the largest pots — crawfish boils, lobster pots, stockpots, big woks. Napoleon lists it around $1,499 MSRP. This is a specialty appliance, not a general-purpose side burner: it needs a bigger cutout, a larger gas supply line, and serious clearances. Buy it only if large-pot cooking is genuinely part of your routine. For everyone else, a conventional dual side burner is the smarter buy.

## Sizing and planning tips

**Plan the cutout before the countertop goes on.** If you're building a stone, tile, or poured-concrete island, the side burner cutout has to be in the plan from day one. Retrofitting a cutout into finished stone is possible but expensive and messy.

**Leave elbow room.** A side burner with a large stockpot on it needs clearance on all sides — crowding it against the grill head or the island edge is a grease-splatter and burn hazard. Most manuals specify minimum clearances; treat them as law, not guidance.

**Check your gas supply.** A side burner draws from the same gas line as your grill. On natural gas, make sure the line is sized for the grill's total BTU plus the burner's — an undersized line starves everything downstream and weakens the flame. Our guide to [natural gas vs propane](/posts/natural-gas-vs-propane-grill/) covers supply sizing in more detail, and if you're running a new gas line, read up on [installation costs](/posts/natural-gas-line-installation-cost/) before you budget.

**Think about the cover habit.** Every side burner ships with a stainless cover — use it. A covered burner stays clean and dry; an uncovered one becomes a leaf collector within a week.

**Ventilation isn't usually required** for a side burner the way it is for the grill head, but if your island sits under a covered structure, read the [outdoor kitchen ventilation guide](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-ventilation-guide/) — the rules for the grill head under a roof still apply.

## Do you actually need one?

If you grill burgers and brats and nothing else, probably not. But if your cookouts involve sides, sauces, or anything in a pot, a side burner transforms the island from a grill with furniture around it into a real kitchen. Budget-wise, it's one of the cheapest appliances in the island — far less than a fridge, sink, or storage module — and for many owners it ends up being the second-most-used burner after the grill itself. Factor it into your [outdoor kitchen cost breakdown](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-cost-breakdown/) early, while the stonework is still a sketch.
