A built-in gas grill isn't furniture — it's a fixed gas appliance bolted into a structure you built for it. That changes the rules. The installation clearance, the island framing, the ventilation, and the gas supply all have to meet the manufacturer's instructions and your local code. Get it right and the grill performs better and lasts longer. Get it wrong and you void the warranty, fail inspection, or create a genuine fire hazard. This guide covers the clearance and safety rules that matter most, with real numbers from manufacturer manuals where they apply.

## The first rule: your manual is the code for your grill

Clearance numbers vary between brands and models. One manual may specify 18 inches to combustible construction and 3 inches to noncombustible surfaces; another may demand 26 inches from the back and sides to any structure at all. The Blaze Premium LTE 32's manual, for example, specifies its cutout at roughly 30¾" wide × 21¼" deep × 9½" tall, with an air gap under the unit and defined clearances to combustible and noncombustible construction — figures that differ from the newer LTE+ generation's 30⅝" × 21¼" × 8½" cutout.

That means this guide can teach you what the rules look like and why they exist, but it cannot replace the manual for the specific grill you're buying. Always design the island around the actual model — see [how to choose a built-in grill](/posts/how-to-choose-built-in-grill/) before you pick the head — and keep the manual on site for the inspector. Installation standards in the US generally reference ANSI Z223.1/NFPA 54 (the National Fuel Gas Code); your local code may add requirements on top.

## Combustible vs noncombustible: the distinction that decides everything

Nearly every built-in grill manual draws a line between two kinds of surroundings:

**Noncombustible materials** — concrete, concrete block, brick, stone, steel studs, fiber-cement board. Clearances here are small (a few inches is typical), because the concern is heat management and airflow rather than ignition.

**Combustible materials** — wood framing, plywood, vinyl siding, wooden pergolas and overhangs, composite decking, landscaping timbers. Clearances here are large (on the order of 18 inches for nearby surfaces in some manuals) because grease-fueled flare-ups and sustained high heat can ignite adjacent wood over time.

The island itself must be noncombustible all the way through. Manufacturers are explicit: the structure, island framing, countertops, and adjacent work areas for a built-in grill installation must be built from noncombustible materials only. That is the single most common violation in DIY outdoor kitchens — a beautiful island framed in pressure-treated lumber with stone veneer over it, and a gas grill sitting in a wood box. If a wood frame is unavoidable for structural reasons, some brands allow insulated jackets or liner systems designed for that purpose; use only a listed product rated for your grill, and verify the inspector accepts it.

## Island framing and the cutout

Build the island around the grill, not the other way around. This is the practical sequence that prevents the most expensive mistakes:

1. **Pick the grill first.** Cutout dimensions are not interchangeable between brands or even between generations of the same grill. The Blaze LTE 32's cutout (about 30¾" × 21¼" × 9½") differs from the LTE+ 32's (about 30⅝" × 21¼" × 8½"). Ordering the stone and cutting the opening before the grill arrives is how islands end up wrong.
2. **Frame with steel studs or block.** Most installers use light-gauge steel studs or concrete block with a fiber-cement or stone face. Wood framing is out, per the combustible-material rule above.
3. **Cut the opening to the manual's dimensions, then add a small forgiveness margin.** Napoleon's built-in models, for example, ship with a trim ring designed to cover minor cutout imperfections — but the trim ring covers millimeters, not inches.
4. **Provide combustion airflow.** Blaze manuals require an air gap under the appliance and a vent cut in the front face of the countertop below the control panel. Island venting matters too: trapped heat inside a sealed island cooks wiring, regulators, and hoses. Our [outdoor kitchen ventilation guide](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-ventilation-guide/) covers island vent placement in depth.
5. **Leave service access.** The regulator, shutoff valve, and any propane tank storage need to be reachable for inspection, leak checks, and eventual replacement. A door or removable panel on the island side is standard practice.

## Overhead clearances and vent hoods

A grill under open sky needs no hood. A grill under anything else needs one — or a large enough gap that nothing can be damaged. The guidance from built-in grill manufacturers follows a consistent pattern:

- **Clearance to overhead construction** is measured from the countertop up. Some manuals call for 72 inches (6 feet) of clearance to noncombustible overhead construction when a vent hood is used; others specify a minimum 5-foot gap between the countertop and overhead construction in their install kits.
- **Under combustible overhead construction** (wood soffit, covered pergola roof, enclosed lanai), the area above the cooking surface must be covered with a listed exhaust hood — and the appliance is not to be used under *unprotected* combustible overhead at all.
- **Hood sizing** generally runs: wider than the cooking area of the grill, with at least 1,200 CFM of airflow for outdoor applications. Underpowering the hood means grease-laden vapor lands on the structure instead of being exhausted.
- **Installation must be performed by a qualified professional service technician.** That line appears in installation kits for a reason — hoods, gas, and structure interact, and the inspector checks the whole assembly.

Also account for lid clearance. Several manuals specify roughly 24 inches minimum behind the grill so the hood opens fully. An island built tight against a wall that the hood bangs into is a daily annoyance that should never have been designed in.

## Gas supply: the part you hire out

This is the least negotiable section of the article. Gas work — running or extending a natural gas line, installing the shutoff, sizing the pipe, pressure-testing the system — is licensed-trade work in nearly every US jurisdiction, and the manual will say so. Here's what to know so you can hire intelligently:

- **Pipe sizing follows total BTU.** Your gas run must deliver the grill's full rated demand (plus any side burner or pizza oven on the same branch) at the required pressure, typically measured in inches of water column. The Lion L75000's 90,000 BTU all-in rating and the Napoleon Prestige 500's 76,000 BTU total don't draw that constantly, but the pipe is sized for the peak. Undersize the line and burners starve at full blast — weak sears, uneven heat.
- **Plan for future appliances.** Running the line once for the grill and again next year for a side burner costs far more than sizing the initial run generously. See [natural gas line installation cost](/posts/natural-gas-line-installation-cost/) for real pricing ranges.
- **A shutoff valve is required** within the island or adjacent and must be accessible — never buried behind the stone with no access panel.
- **Leak testing is mandatory** at every connection after install, after tank refills (propane), and periodically after that. The correct method is a soapy-water solution brushed over fittings while the gas is on: bubbles mean a leak. Never use a flame or match to check for leaks.
- **Propane and natural gas are not interchangeable.** Most premium heads ship in separate propane and natural gas versions; field conversion may be possible with a kit or may not be offered at all. Decide before ordering — our [natural gas vs propane](/posts/natural-gas-vs-propane-grill/) comparison covers the running-cost tradeoff.

## The five mistakes that fail inspections (and void warranties)

**1. Wood framing under the grill.** Covered above. Inspectors see it, they flag it. The grill manufacturers' manuals are explicit, and "the veneer is stone" does not make the framing noncombustible.

**2. Cutting the opening before buying the grill.** Cutout dimensions differ by model and generation. A 30-inch cutout cannot be stretched to 32¾ inches without rebuilding the island top.

**3. No island ventilation.** A sealed island traps heat around regulators, hoses, and wiring. Every island needs low and high venting per the ventilation guide.

**4. The gas line sized for the grill only.** You add a [side burner](/posts/best-side-burner-outdoor-kitchen/) in year two, the burners starve, and the plumber runs a second line through your finished island. Size once.

**5. Skipping the permit.** Gas work without a permit can void your homeowner's insurance coverage for the incident you most need coverage for. The permit costs a fraction of the island; the inspection takes an hour.

## Pre-build checklist

- [ ] Grill model selected and manual's cutout dimensions recorded
- [ ] Island framed in noncombustible material (steel studs or block)
- [ ] Clearance to nearby combustibles verified against the manual
- [ ] Overhead situation resolved: open sky, or vent hood with proper CFM and overhead clearance
- [ ] Island venting planned (intake low, exhaust high)
- [ ] Gas line sized for total BTU of grill + future appliances, by a licensed pro
- [ ] Shutoff valve accessible; service access panel planned
- [ ] Permit pulled and inspection scheduled
- [ ] Leak test performed on every connection before first ignition
