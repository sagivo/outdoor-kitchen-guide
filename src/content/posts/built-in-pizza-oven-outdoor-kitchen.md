A pizza oven is the appliance that turns an outdoor kitchen from a grill station into a destination. But unlike a grill head — which drops into a standard cutout — a pizza oven brings real weight, real chimney requirements, and real decisions about fuel and permanence. There are four distinct routes to a pizza oven in your island, and they differ wildly in cost, complexity, and commitment. This guide lays out each one with the numbers that matter, so you can pick the right build for how you'll actually cook.

## The four routes, ranked by commitment

**Route 1: Countertop placement.** Set a countertop-rated oven directly on your island's stone or concrete counter. This is the simplest path: no chimney, no custom surround, no mason. It works with portable ovens like the [Ooni Koda 16](/grills/ooni-koda-16/) (40 lb, propane) or a semi-permanent [Gozney Dome S1](/grills/gozney-dome-s1/) (58 lb) that effectively lives where you put it. The critical constraint: the countertop must be noncombustible — granite, concrete, stainless, or porcelain — and structurally rated for the oven's weight. An Alfa Futuro 2 Pizze at 242 pounds sitting on a laminate-wrapped island is a structural and fire problem, not a pizza station.

**Route 2: Insert-style built-in.** These are finished ovens designed to drop into a pre-built surround — think of them like a built-in grill head, but much heavier and taller. Brands in this space include Alfa Forni, Chicago Brick Oven, Lynx, and Fire Magic. They typically need a dedicated flue or chimney, which your island design has to route through its finish. Inserts are the fastest way to a truly integrated look, and they carry manufacturer warranties.

**Route 3: Prefab dome kit.** Brands like Forno Bravo and Tuscan Chef (and Chicago Brick Oven's kit line) ship a pre-cast refractory dome or modular assembly; you or your mason build the surround, insulation wrap, and finish around it. This sits between inserts and full custom builds on cost and effort. A Forno Bravo Casa-series kit can be installed in as little as two days, but the kit is only the inner shell — the base, insulation, and finish are on you.

**Route 4: Fully custom masonry.** A mason builds the entire oven from scratch using refractory brick or block. Maximum design freedom, and potentially lower material costs where local labor is affordable — but you're buying many skilled days, and the project timeline stretches to weeks.

Cost reality check: insert units run roughly $1,800 to $7,500 (Chicago Brick Oven kits $2,899–$7,999; Forno Bravo kits $2,905–$6,545; Lynx around $5,949). Installation — the reinforced base, chimney, and surrounding build — adds roughly $2,000 to $8,000+. A complete mid-range build typically lands between $6,000 and $14,000; elaborate custom masonry surrounds run $20,000 to $30,000 or more. National averages blend modest DIY installs down to about $5,000 all-in, but a built-in is not a $500 project.

## Structure first: the island has to be built for the oven

This is where the grill-installation rules carry over and get stricter. Like a built-in grill, everything around the oven must be noncombustible — steel framing, block, or masonry. If you built your island around the [grill installation clearances](/posts/built-in-grill-installation-clearances-safety/) rules, the pizza-oven island follows the same playbook with heavier loads.

Weight drives the design. The Chicago Brick Oven CBO-750 ships as a 750-pound pre-cast refractory cement dome that holds 900°F for hours and bakes a Neapolitan pizza in 60–90 seconds. Dome-kit shipments can weigh around 1,000 pounds and sometimes arrive by liftgate truck, with a forklift needed to get the crate to the backyard. Even the "light" countertop options are serious loads: Alfa's Futuro 2 Pizze weighs 242 pounds without a base. Get the oven's published weight and footprint before the island is framed — retrofitting structural support after the stone is on is exactly the kind of mistake that costs five figures.

Modular kitchen systems have simplified this for countertop-style installs. Fontana Forni's Elysia outdoor kitchen modules, for example, are built with access holes at the rear top corner of each panel so gas and electric can be routed through them, and the company designs its ovens to need only about 1 inch of clearance on the top, bottom, and all sides. Their ovens aren't permanently affixed — they sit on oven base modules, which means you can move the oven if you redesign. If you're shopping countertop placement, that's the kind of detail to verify with your manufacturer: exact clearance, exact weight, and whether the oven is meant to be removable.

## Fuel: gas, wood, or both — decide before you build the island

The fuel decision is structural, because it determines what your island has to accommodate. See the [pizza oven fuel guide](/posts/pizza-oven-fuel-guide/) for the full comparison, but the built-in-specific version is this:

**Natural gas / propane** is the low-friction choice. Gas ovens reach temperature in 20–45 minutes, run about $1 to $1.25 per hour on natural gas, and need minimal management — which means the oven actually gets used on weeknights, not just Saturdays. Your island needs a gas line stubbed to the oven location. Fontana Forni's modular approach shows what's possible: the same cabinetry can feed propane or natural gas to the oven, or hold wood storage below a wood-fired unit. If you're running a [natural gas line to the island](/posts/natural-gas-line-installation-cost/) anyway for the grill, routing a branch to the oven is marginal added cost at the rough-in stage and painful later.

**Wood** needs fire management and a 45–90 minute preheat, but produces the smoke character and char that gas can't replicate. Wood-fired inserts and masonry ovens also demand more of the chimney design, because a live flame throws more sparks and needs proper draft. Many experienced owners say they'd go dual-fuel if starting over — ovens from Alfa Forni and Forno Bravo offer gas/wood or gas/hybrid configurations in one unit.

**Dual-fuel** is the hedge. It costs more up front but ends the debate: gas for Tuesday, wood for the party. If your island can only accommodate one fuel route, though, make the call based on honesty about your habits — the best oven is the one that gets hot twice a week.

## The chimney question

This is the single detail most first-time builders underestimate. Insert-style and masonry ovens typically need a dedicated flue or chimney — it's fundamental to how they draft. The chimney has to pass through your island's finish structure, which means the island design must include a chimney chase before anything gets built. Countertop gas ovens like the Koda 16 or Dome S1 vent out the front and skip the chimney entirely, which is part of why Route 1 is so much simpler.

Two practical consequences: first, if your island sits under a roof, pergola cover, or overhang, the chimney plan interacts with your [ventilation strategy](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-ventilation-guide/) — a pizza oven under a combustible structure without proper clearance and exhaust design is the same category of problem as a grill under one. Second, chimney height affects draft: wood ovens in particular need the flue to clear nearby structures, so check the manual's chimney requirements the way you'd check a grill's clearance table.

## Sizing: how much oven do you actually need?

Oven size is a cooking-surface decision, not a prestige decision. A few real reference points:

- The **Chicago Brick Oven CBO-750** offers a roughly 750-square-inch cooking hearth (about 32" × 24") — enough for two 12-inch pizzas at once, or one pizza plus a cast-iron pan of vegetables. Its DIY-kit sibling line uses a 38" × 28" cooking surface and cooks 2–3 pizzas simultaneously.
- **Alfa's Futuro 2 Pizze** gives you a 27.5" × 15.7" cooking surface (up to two pizzas at a time), reaches 1000°F in about 30 minutes, and bakes in roughly 90 seconds.
- The **Gozney Dome S1** cooks 16-inch pizzas — the step up that matters when you're feeding a crowd and halves your number of launches versus a 12-inch oven.

The pattern from the [best pizza ovens roundup](/posts/best-pizza-ovens-2026/): 12-inch ovens suit couples and small families; 16-inch decks suit entertainers. A 750-square-inch hearth is a party machine, but it also preheats longer and costs more to feed. Size for your actual crowd, not your imagined one.

## Gas line, permits, and the order of operations

Build in this order and you avoid the expensive re-dos:

1. **Pick the oven and fuel first.** The oven's manual dictates weight, footprint, clearance, and chimney specs — everything the island designer needs.
2. **Rough in utilities before the island is built.** Gas stub-outs, electrical (for ignition, lighting, or a rotisserie-style motor), and any drainage. Retrofitting utilities after stone and stucco is where budgets die.
3. **Build the island and chimney chase around the oven's dimensions.** Cutouts for pizza-oven inserts aren't interchangeable between brands any more than grill cutouts are.
4. **Set the oven, connect the chimney, then finish.** Stucco, stone veneer, or tile goes last — after the oven is confirmed in place and the chimney drafts properly.
5. **Permits.** Gas line work, structural work, and anything under a roof often need permits. The [outdoor kitchen cost breakdown](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-cost-breakdown/) covers where permits typically land in the budget — treat the oven like the gas appliance it is, and the inspector like a partner.

## When the countertop route is the right call

Not every outdoor kitchen needs a masonry dome. The countertop route (Route 1) is the right call when: your island is already built, you want the oven running this weekend, you cook pizza mostly on weeknights (gas convenience wins), or your budget caps at a few thousand. A Dome S1 at roughly $1,400–$1,600 on a granite counter is a genuinely excellent pizza setup for a fraction of a masonry build. The masonry route is the right call when the oven is the centerpiece of a whole-kitchen build, you want wood fire, and you're already committing to the structural work. And if you land somewhere in between, an insert-style unit gives you the built-in look with manufacturer-engineered parts and a warranty, without hiring a mason for a month.

The oven should match the kitchen's ambition. The one mistake is designing the island first and shopping for the oven that fits whatever space is left — that's how you end up with a beautiful island and an oven you use twice a year. Pick the oven, then build it a home.
