The grill is bought, the island is planned, and then comes the quote that makes people blink: running the gas line. Natural gas is the fuel of choice for most built-in outdoor kitchens — no tanks to swap, no running out mid-cookout, cheaper fuel per BTU — but the line has to be installed by a licensed professional, permitted, and sized for the grill's full output. Here's what that actually costs, what drives the price up or down, and how to make sure the line you pay for can feed everything in your island.

If you're still weighing fuels, our [natural gas vs propane](/posts/natural-gas-vs-propane-grill/) guide covers the long-term trade-offs in full.

## The short answer: what most people pay

For a professionally installed natural gas line to a grill or built-in outdoor kitchen, **most homeowners spend $900–$1,500** all-in — pipe, fittings, quick-disconnect hookup, permit, and labor — when the island sits reasonably close to an existing gas supply.

Real-world examples from homeowner reports illustrate the range:

- A simple hookup tapping an existing stub, adding a quick disconnect and permit: **$425–$725**
- A plumber running ~40 feet of pipe under a concrete patio to a built-in kitchen: **~$1,410**
- A gas line with quick disconnect on a pool patio about 15 feet from the supply: **~$1,835**
- A brand-new line from the street serving a grill and firepit area: **$3,200**

The broad pattern: the closer your island is to an existing gas source, the closer you land to the low end. Longer, buried, or concrete-crossing runs push the price up fast.

## What drives the cost

**Distance and routing.** This is the number-one cost driver. Installed piping typically costs around **$20 per linear foot**, and every obstacle adds labor: trenching through soil is cheaper than cutting and repairing a concrete patio, and going around a pool or under a driveway multiplies the complexity. A 5-foot extension to an existing stub might cost $425; a 40-foot buried run under a patio can triple that.

**Starting from a stub vs. from scratch.** The cheapest scenario is a house that already has a natural gas stub or capped line near the patio — many newer homes do. The plumber taps it, adds a shutoff valve and quick disconnect, and you're done. The expensive scenario is no existing line anywhere near: the run comes from the gas meter (or the street, in extreme cases), with more pipe, more trenching, and possibly a meter upgrade.

**Meter capacity.** Your gas meter and the service line feeding it have a finite capacity, measured in BTU per hour. If the meter is already feeding a furnace, water heater, and range, adding a 60,000–75,000 BTU grill head — plus a side burner or fire feature — may exceed it. The utility can upgrade the meter, but that's a separate job on their schedule and their price list. Have your plumber do the BTU load calculation before you assume the existing meter is fine.

**Pipe material.** Most residential gas runs use black iron pipe above ground and either black iron, galvanized, or corrugated stainless steel tubing (CSST) with protective jacketing for buried or outdoor runs, depending on local code. Your utility may prohibit certain materials — this isn't a DIY materials decision; the licensed installer picks what's allowed in your area.

**Permits and inspection.** Permits for gas work typically run **$200–$500** depending on the municipality, and the job must pass a pressure test and inspection before the gas is turned on. Skipping the permit is false economy: unpermitted gas work can void your homeowners insurance and becomes a liability when you sell.

**Conversion vs. new grill.** If you already own a propane grill, a natural gas conversion kit runs roughly **$100–$250** — but the grill must be approved for conversion, and many manufacturers void the warranty on converted units. Most people building a built-in kitchen buy the natural gas version of the grill head outright, which sidesteps the question entirely.

## The BTU math your installer will do

Your gas line must feed the *total* demand of everything connected to it. A typical built-in grill head runs 60,000–75,000 BTU. Add a side burner (10,000–24,000 BTU depending on the unit) and you're at 70,000–100,000 BTU on one branch. If the island also feeds a fire feature or patio heater, the total climbs further.

Undersized pipe is the classic mistake: the line delivers less gas than the appliances demand, and the symptoms look like a defective grill — weak flames, uneven heat, burners that sputter on high. When you get quotes, confirm the plumber is sizing pipe for your grill's *rated* BTU plus every other appliance on the run, not just "a grill." Pipe that is one size too big costs marginally more; pipe that is one size too small costs you a re-do.

## The installation process, step by step

1. **Plan the route.** Mark where the island sits and where the nearest gas supply is. The shortest, straightest buried route wins.
2. **Call 811 before you dig.** Utility locating is free and legally required in most states — cutting a buried electric, fiber, or water line turns a gas job into a disaster.
3. **Get quotes from licensed plumbers or gas fitters.** Two or three quotes is standard; confirm each quote includes the permit, pressure test, shutoff valve, and quick-disconnect.
4. **Check meter capacity.** Your plumber should calculate total BTU load and confirm with the utility whether the meter needs an upgrade.
5. **Install, pressure test, inspect.** The installer pressurizes the new line and holds it to prove there are no leaks, then the inspector signs off.
6. **Connect and leak-check the grill.** The quick-disconnect makes seasonal removal easy, and every new connection gets a bubble test with soapy water.

Simple hookups are often a half-day job. New buried runs with meter work and inspection can run one to three days.

## Running the payback math

Natural gas costs substantially less per BTU than propane, and you never pay the propane surcharge of tank swaps or refills. The exact payback depends on your local gas rates and how much you grill, but for a household that grills several times a week, the $900–$1,500 line installation typically pays for itself in a few grilling seasons — faster if the same line also feeds a side burner, fire pit, or patio heater. Against the [full cost of the outdoor kitchen](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-cost-breakdown/), the gas line is a mid-size line item that pays you back every year you own the island.

## Should you run the line if you're on propane now?

Ask one question: how many tanks do you burn through per season, and how much does each fill cost? A heavy griller on propane can spend $200–$400 per season on tank fills, with the added annoyance of a tank dying mid-cookout. If the math says you'll spend $1,000+ on propane over five years anyway, the gas line is close to break-even on fuel alone — and the convenience (endless fuel, no tank logistics, no tank stored on the island) is free. If you grill twice a month in summer and that's it, propane may remain the rational choice, and our [fuel comparison](/posts/natural-gas-vs-propane-grill/) will help you decide.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Undersizing the line** for the grill's full rated BTU plus future appliances. Size for what the island *will* have, not just what it has on day one.
- **Skipping the permit.** Saves $200–$500, risks your insurance and resale.
- **Routing above ground where it shouldn't be.** Buried lines need proper depth and protection; above-ground runs need to be protected from physical damage and UV where applicable.
- **Forgetting the shutoff.** Every island needs an accessible gas shutoff valve, plus the quick-disconnect at the grill. Confirm both are in the quote.
- **Adding the line after the patio is finished.** If you're building the island, trench the gas line *before* the concrete or pavers go in — doing it afterward means cutting, patching, and a much bigger bill.
