Picking between a natural gas and a propane grill is one of the first decisions in any outdoor kitchen build — and one of the hardest to undo. The grill heads are nearly identical, the cooking results are nearly identical, but the infrastructure behind them is completely different. Here's how to choose, with the real costs attached.

## The short answer

**Choose natural gas** if your house already has a gas line (or you can run one affordably), you grill at least weekly, and your grill will live permanently in the island. You will never run out of fuel mid-cook, and the running cost is a fraction of propane.

**Choose propane** if you rent, can't easily run a gas line, want to keep the build simple, or might rearrange the patio later. A 20 lb tank and a regulator get you grilling the same afternoon.

One non-negotiable rule: **most built-in heads are not field-convertible between fuels.** The orifices, regulators, and valves differ. You order the natural gas version or the propane version, and that's what you have. So decide now.

## Cooking performance: effectively a tie

Propane technically carries more energy per cubic foot than natural gas, and propane burners can run slightly hotter on paper. In practice, manufacturers engineer each version to hit the same temperatures — a natural gas [Blaze Premium LTE 32](/grills/blaze-premium-lte-32/) and its propane twin sear steaks the same way. Reviewers and pitmasters consistently report no meaningful difference in heat-up time, max temperature, or evenness between the two versions of the same head.

Flavor is the other myth to kill: neither fuel changes how food tastes. Gas burns clean; the "grilled" flavor comes from drippings hitting hot metal and from smoke you add yourself. If someone tells you propane "tastes better," they are tasting their marinade.

## Cost comparison: fuel, installation, and the long game

**Propane running cost.** A 20 lb tank holds about 4.6 gallons of propane and typically delivers 18–20 hours of grilling on a mid-size head. Tank exchanges run roughly $20–$30 depending on your area. If you grill twice a week year-round, expect to burn through a tank every month or two — a few hundred dollars a year in fuel, plus the ritual of noticing you're low at the worst possible moment.

**Natural gas running cost.** Utility natural gas is usually dramatically cheaper per BTU — commonly half to a third the cost of tank propane. That same twice-a-week griller might spend well under $100 a year on fuel. The savings compound: over a decade of heavy grilling, natural gas can save you $1,000+ in fuel alone.

**Installation cost.** This is where propane wins. A propane setup needs a tank, a hose, and a regulator — essentially zero installation. Natural gas needs a licensed gas fitter to run a line to the island, plus permits in most jurisdictions. Budget roughly $500–$2,000+ depending on distance, trenching, and local labor. Our [outdoor kitchen cost breakdown](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-cost-breakdown/) has the full line-item picture, and the [build cost estimator](/tools/build-cost-estimator/) lets you model it.

**The breakeven.** If running the line costs $1,200 and you save ~$150/year on fuel, you break even in about eight years — and you get the convenience of never swapping tanks from day one. If the line only costs $500 (short run, easy trench), the math is a no-brainer for anyone who grills regularly.

## Convenience and lifestyle

**Natural gas wins on:** never running out mid-cook, no tank storage, no tank swaps, cleaner island design (no tank compartment needed), slightly lower emissions.

**Propane wins on:** zero installation, portability (move the grill, take it to a tailgate), renter-friendliness, works anywhere including places without gas service, and simpler permitting.

There is one more scenario worth naming: **houses with large in-ground propane tanks.** If you heat with propane from a buried tank, your grill can often tie into that supply with the right regulator — you get piped-fuel convenience without a utility gas line. Ask your propane supplier; it is a common setup in rural areas.

## Safety and code notes

Both fuels are safe when installed correctly, and both are dangerous when installed badly. A few rules that apply to both:

- Gas work (running lines, making connections) should be done or inspected by a licensed professional. This is not a DIY YouTube project.
- Never enclose a propane tank in an unvented compartment. Islands housing tanks need dedicated ventilation openings top and bottom — check your grill manual for the exact requirements.
- Keep a spray bottle of soapy water handy: bubbles at a connection mean a leak. Or buy a dedicated gas leak detector for a few dollars.
- Local codes may require shutoff valves within a certain distance of the grill and specific clearances from structures. Your permit office or gas fitter will know.

## Which fuel for which buyer?

- **The permanent outdoor kitchen, homeowner, gas line available:** natural gas. Lower running cost, zero fuel logistics, cleaner build.
- **The renter or the "maybe I'll rearrange" patio:** propane. No commitments, no permits, grill today.
- **The rural home with a buried propane tank:** plumbed propane — the best of both.
- **The high-BTU obsessive:** propane has a small theoretical edge in max heat, but on a quality head like the [Napoleon Prestige 500](/grills/napoleon-prestige-500-built-in/) you will never notice. Don't let this decide for you.
