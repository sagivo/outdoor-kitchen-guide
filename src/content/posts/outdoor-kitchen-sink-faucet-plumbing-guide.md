An outdoor kitchen sink is the feature people underestimate and then use constantly. It is where you rinse vegetables, fill a pot without walking back inside, wash your hands after handling raw chicken, and soak the tongs you forgot about. It is also the only part of the build that brings pressurized water and a drain into a structure full of gas lines, electricity, and hot metal — which is why the sink deserves more planning than its size suggests. Get the sink type, water supply, drainage, and freeze protection right, and it quietly earns its keep for a decade. Get any one of them wrong, and you get a frozen pipe, a smelly dry well, or a drain that backs up at the worst moment.

This guide covers sink types and materials, hot- and cold-water options, the three drainage routes and the rules that govern them, freeze protection, faucet choices, placement, and rough costs. It assumes you are building a permanent island; if you are still deciding how the whole project fits together, start with the [outdoor kitchen cost breakdown](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-cost-breakdown/) and the [countertop materials guide](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-countertop-materials/) before you commit to a sink cutout.

## Sink types: bowl count, size, and mounting

Most outdoor sinks are single-bowl stainless units, and for good reason. A single deep bowl swallows a half-sheet pan, a stockpot, and a cutting board without the divider of a double bowl getting in the way. Double bowls make sense only if you genuinely wash and rinse in two stages outside — for example, if the outdoor kitchen is the main summer kitchen and dishes are washed there nightly. For most households, the second bowl becomes a drying rack that stole prep space.

Size follows the island, not the other way around. A bar or prep sink of about 13–16 inches wide suits a compact island or a drinks station. A main outdoor sink of about 15–21 inches wide and 8–10 inches deep handles real cooking work. Anything wider than about 21 inches needs a long counter run to justify it; on a short island, a big sink leaves you with nowhere to set down a cutting board. In a [small outdoor kitchen](/posts/small-outdoor-kitchen-ideas/), a compact single bowl with a cutting-board cover is often the smarter buy than a full-size sink, because the cover turns the basin back into counter space when it is not in use.

Mounting style matters for both looks and installation. A drop-in (topmount) sink sits in a cutout with a visible rim; it is the simplest to install, the easiest to replace, and the most forgiving of an imperfect cutout. An undermount sink is fixed beneath the countertop, so the counter edge shows and crumbs wipe straight in; it looks cleaner but needs a solid countertop material that can carry the mounting hardware, and the cutout has to be precise. A universal-mount sink can be installed either way, which is useful if you have not finalized the countertop yet. Whichever style you choose, give the countertop fabricator the sink template before the slab is cut — the same rule that applies to grill cutouts — and confirm the cabinet below is deep and wide enough for the bowl, the drain, and the trap.

Some outdoor sink kits bundle extras: a faucet, a soap dispenser, a cutting board, a drain stopper, or a stainless cover. Bundles are convenient, but judge the sink and the faucet separately. A good basin with a cheap faucet that corrodes in a year is a bad deal, and a faucet you love is easy to buy on its own. Covers are worth having on any exposed sink: they keep leaves, rain, and grill ash out of the basin and give you back a flat surface.

## Materials: why stainless steel dominates, and which grade

Outdoor sinks are overwhelmingly stainless steel because it tolerates water, sun, grease, and temperature swings without the sealing and cracking worries of stone or composite. Within stainless, the grade and gauge are the two numbers that matter.

Grade 304 stainless is the standard for outdoor kitchen equipment and suits most inland climates. Grade 316 stainless adds molybdenum for extra resistance to chlorides, which makes it the better choice near the ocean, near a saltwater pool, or anywhere de-icing salt is tracked onto the patio. If you are on the coast, matching the sink grade to the grill matters: a 316 sink next to a 304 grill is fine, but a 304 sink in a salt-air location will show tea-staining and corrosion sooner. Check the grade before you buy rather than assuming every stainless sink is the same.

Gauge measures thickness, and lower is thicker: 16-gauge stainless is heavier and quieter than 18-gauge, and it resists denting from a dropped cast-iron pan. For an outdoor sink that will take real use, 16-gauge is worth the premium; 18-gauge is acceptable for light prep use. Sound-dampening pads or coating on the underside also help, because a thin stainless bowl rings loudly under running water. Avoid painted or coated finishes that are not rated for outdoor exposure, and be wary of black or colored stainless finishes unless you accept that scratches will show the bare metal beneath.

Other materials exist but are niche outdoors. Granite composite sinks look good and resist stains, but they are heavier, need a compatible countertop, and are less forgiving of hot pans. Fireclay and ceramic sinks chip if a heavy pot is dropped in them and are rare in outdoor-rated lines. Copper develops a patina that some people love and others do not, and it needs care that most outdoor kitchens do not get. For a first outdoor sink, stainless in the right grade and gauge is the low-regret choice.

## Water supply: cold-only, extended hot water, or point-of-use heat

There are three honest ways to feed an outdoor sink, and the right one depends on how much washing you will actually do outside.

Cold-only is the simplest and cheapest. A single cold line from the house, with an indoor shutoff and a drain-down point, gives you rinsing, pot-filling, and hand-washing. It needs the least plumbing, the least winterizing, and no water heater. Many outdoor kitchens never need more, especially if the indoor kitchen is a few steps away and heavy dishwashing happens inside. If your budget is tight or your climate freezes hard, start cold-only; adding hot water later is a second plumbing project, but a cold-only sink that works beats a hot-and-cold sink that freezes and splits.

Extending the home hot-water line is the most capable option. The plumber runs hot and cold lines from the house to the island, insulates them, and provides an indoor shutoff for both. You get unlimited hot water at the same temperature as indoors, which is ideal if you wash greasy pans and tools outside. The downsides are cost, trenching or routing through walls, and a longer winterizing job, because both lines must drain fully. Long runs also waste water while you wait for hot water to arrive, so ask the plumber about the run length and whether a recirculation loop makes sense for your layout.

A point-of-use heater heats water at the island instead of piping it from the house. The common forms are a small electric tankless unit or a compact tank heater mounted in the island cabinet, fed by a cold line only. This avoids a long hot-water run and can be cheaper to install than extending house plumbing, but it needs electrical capacity in the island, it must be rated for the location and protected from weather, and a tankless unit must be sized to the faucet flow — an undersized heater gives lukewarm water at full flow. Never install a water heater, or any electrical device, inside a sealed island cabinet without checking ventilation, clearances, and the manufacturer's instructions, and use a licensed plumber and electrician for the work.

Whichever route you take, two details are non-negotiable. First, the supply must have an indoor shutoff valve that is easy to reach, so you can isolate the outdoor kitchen without shutting off the house. Second, the outdoor supply needs backflow protection appropriate to your local code — typically a vacuum breaker or backflow preventer — so that sink water can never be siphoned back into the drinking-water supply. A plumber will know what your jurisdiction requires; do not skip it to save a fitting.

A fourth option, a garden-hose feed to a freestanding sink, is a temporary solution, not a permanent kitchen. It works for a portable setup or a rental, but it has no freeze protection, no proper backflow control, and no hot water. Treat it as a stopgap while you decide whether a plumbed sink is worth the investment.

## Drainage: tie-in, dry well, or bucket — and the rules that decide

Drainage is where outdoor sink projects go wrong, because the easy options have hidden rules. There are three routes, in descending order of capability.

A tie-in to the house drain is the gold standard. The sink drain connects to the home sewer or septic system, with a trap and venting like any indoor sink. It handles grease, food particles, soap, and volume without special rules about what goes down the drain, because the waste goes where all household waste goes. It is also the most expensive and disruptive to install, since it needs a drain line run to the house, proper slope, and venting. If you are already trenching for water and gas lines, adding the drain while the ground is open is far cheaper than doing it later. If a tie-in is feasible, take it — it removes an entire category of restrictions from how you use the sink.

A dry well is a covered, stone-filled pit that lets drain water seep into the surrounding soil. It is a legitimate option in some jurisdictions for light greywater, but it comes with conditions that surprise many builders. First, local rules vary widely and some jurisdictions prohibit dry wells for sink drainage altogether, so checking with the local building or health department is a required first step, not a formality. Second, dry wells commonly require setbacks — often at least 10 feet from the house foundation, and commonly 50 to 100 feet from a potable water well — and they must be built in permeable soil, not clay. Third, what goes down the drain is restricted: grease, food scraps, and harsh chemicals can clog the pit, cause odors, and reclassify the discharge, so a dry-well sink is a rinse-and-handwash sink, not a dishwashing sink. If you cook greasy food and expect to wash pans outside, a dry well is the wrong drainage choice. A dry well also needs occasional attention: if drainage slows or odors appear, the pit may be saturated or clogged and may need professional attention.

A bucket or container under the sink is the simplest drainage of all, and the most limited. The drain empties into a removable container that you empty by hand — into the house drain, onto a suitable garden area where allowed, or into a compost or disposal route that follows local rules. It costs almost nothing and needs no permit in most places, but it is messy, it fills fast during a cookout, and forgetting to empty it means an overflowing cabinet. It suits a lightly used prep sink, a portable setup, or a temporary installation while a permanent drain is planned. It does not suit a sink that will see heavy use or greasy washing, and the container must be emptied before it can overflow or attract pests.

Whatever drainage you choose, do not let sink water simply run onto the patio or into the yard. Surface discharge of kitchen wastewater is restricted or prohibited in many jurisdictions, it creates odors and pests, and it can stain or undermine the patio. If a contractor suggests an open drain to daylight, get a second opinion and a code check first.

## Freeze protection and winterizing

Water expands when it freezes, and a full pipe, valve, or trap will split on the first hard freeze. In any climate that sees freezing temperatures, the sink must be designed to be emptied, not just insulated. Insulation slows freezing; it does not prevent it in a sustained cold snap.

The winterizing routine, done before the first freeze, is: shut off the indoor supply valve that feeds the outdoor kitchen; open the outdoor faucet and any drain valves and let the lines empty; blow out the lines with compressed air if the system was not built to drain fully by gravity; clear the sink trap or protect it with RV-rated antifreeze where the manufacturer and local practice allow; and leave the faucets open through winter so no vacuum traps water in the lines. Cover the basin so rain and melting snow do not refill the system. The same routine applies to any ice maker or refrigerator water line in the island — anything that holds water is at risk, while the gas grill itself can keep being used through winter.

Design choices make winterizing easier or harder. A dedicated indoor shutoff with a drain-down valve, a valve box buried between the house and the island, and lines that slope toward the drain point all make the annual job a ten-minute task. Lines that sag, trap water in low spots, or have no drain valve turn winterizing into a guessing game. If you are building in a freeze climate, ask the plumber to show you the shutoff, the drain valve, and the drain-down path before the island is closed up — and test the routine once before the first winter, while a mistake is still cheap.

In mild climates that freeze only rarely, the same routine applies on forecast nights: shut off, drain, and open the faucets before the freeze, rather than discovering a split line in the morning. A single hard freeze is enough to crack a valve. If you travel during winter, winterize before you leave rather than trusting a forecast.

## Faucet choices for outdoor use

The faucet is the part you touch every time, and outdoor conditions punish cheap faucets. Look for a faucet rated for outdoor or commercial use, made from 304 or 316 stainless steel or solid brass, with ceramic disc cartridges rather than rubber washers. Ceramic discs last longer and drip less. A pull-down or pull-out sprayer is genuinely useful outdoors for rinsing large pans and the basin itself, but check that the hose and spray head are rated for outdoor exposure and that the sprayer retracts cleanly — a dangling spray head collects grime.

Single-hole faucets are the simplest and most common on outdoor sinks; widespread or bridge faucets suit larger sinks and a more traditional look. A high-arc spout clears tall pots; a low-arc spout splashes less in a shallow bowl. Match the faucet hole count to the sink or the countertop drilling before you buy, and confirm the faucet includes or can accept a soap dispenser or an air gap if your local code requires one for the dishwasher or disposal connection.

Freeze resistance matters even for the faucet. A frost-free or freeze-resistant faucet design, a faucet that can be drained with the rest of the system, and a habit of leaving it open through winter all help. In a hard-freeze climate, some builders use a separate outdoor faucet with a long stem that shuts off inside the heated space; ask your plumber whether that design suits your island. Whatever faucet you choose, keep the finish simple: brushed stainless hides water spots and fingerprints better than polished chrome, and it matches the grill and the countertop hardware.

## Placement: the sink in the cooking workflow

Place the sink where the work happens, not where it looks symmetrical. The classic outdoor workflow runs grill to prep to sink: raw food comes out of the house or the refrigerator, gets prepped on the counter, goes on the grill, and comes back to a landing zone. The sink belongs on the prep side of that loop, within a step or two of the main counter, so you can rinse, wash hands, and set down a hot pan without crossing the cook's path.

Keep a clear landing zone on at least one side of the sink — about 18–24 inches of counter is a good planning figure — for a cutting board, a colander, or a pan waiting to be washed. Do not put the sink directly against the grill head: splashing water and a hot grill are a bad combination, and the sink eats counter space that the grill landing zone needs. A run of counter between the grill and the sink, or a sink on an adjacent leg of an L-shaped island, keeps both zones usable at once. Check the [grill installation clearance guide](/posts/built-in-grill-installation-clearances-safety/) for the noncombustible and clearance rules that govern everything around the grill head, and keep the sink cabinet clear of gas lines and electrical junctions that belong elsewhere in the island.

Think about splash and drainage at the countertop, too. The countertop around the sink should slope very slightly toward the basin, the sink rim or undermount joint must be sealed against water, and the cabinet below must be able to tolerate occasional dampness — stainless or marine-grade cabinet materials, or at minimum a sealed cabinet floor and a leak check after installation. The countertop material choice interacts here as well: a non-porous surface such as porcelain or stainless around the sink is more forgiving of standing water than a porous stone that needs frequent sealing, as detailed in the [countertop materials guide](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-countertop-materials/). If a side burner is part of the plan, keep it on the cooking side of the island rather than crowding the sink; see the [side burner guide](/posts/best-side-burner-outdoor-kitchen/) for how that appliance fits the layout.

Finally, plan for trash and storage near the sink. A pull-out trash bin or a waste drawer within arm's reach keeps peelings and packaging off the counter, and a small cabinet for soap, brushes, and a dish towel keeps the sink area from becoming a clutter trap. These are small decisions that determine whether the sink gets used daily or avoided.

## Rough costs: sink, faucet, plumbing, and drainage

Costs vary widely by region, access, and whether the ground is already open for other utilities, so treat these as rough planning ranges and get itemized local quotes. Check current prices before you buy, since sink and faucet pricing moves with the market.

A basic stainless drop-in sink runs roughly $150–$400. An outdoor-rated 304 stainless sink in 16-gauge, in the 15–21 inch range, typically runs roughly $400–$700. A full outdoor sink kit that bundles a faucet, soap dispenser, cutting board, and cover commonly runs roughly $700–$1,050 or more. A 316 stainless sink for coastal use costs more than the equivalent 304 model. The faucet alone, if bought separately, commonly runs roughly $100–$400 for an outdoor-rated model, with pull-down sprayers at the higher end.

Plumbing labor is usually the larger cost. Running water supply lines to the island, installing the shutoff and drain-down valves, and connecting the drain commonly runs roughly $800–$2,500 depending on distance, trenching, and access, and a drain tie-in to the house system adds more. A dry well, where allowed, adds excavation and stone but avoids the drain-line run; a bucket drain adds almost nothing. Winterizing by a plumber, if you do not do it yourself, is a modest annual service call. Run the full project numbers through the [build cost estimator](/tools/build-cost-estimator/) so the sink, plumbing, and drainage are priced as part of the whole build rather than as an afterthought — a sink added late, after the countertop is cut and the ground is closed, costs far more than one planned from the start.

## FAQ

**Can I use an indoor kitchen sink outside?** Only if it is rated for outdoor exposure. An indoor sink may use a finish, mounting, or faucet that corrodes, and its warranty may not cover outdoor use. An outdoor-rated stainless sink in the right grade is the safer buy, and it is usually not much more expensive than a comparable indoor model.

**Does an outdoor sink need a vent?** A sink tied into the house drain needs venting like any indoor sink, which is one more reason to use a plumber for a tie-in. A bucket drain needs no vent, and a dry well follows local rules that your building department or plumber will confirm. Do not assume a short drain run can skip venting.

**What should never go down an outdoor sink drain?** Grease, food scraps, and harsh chemicals should never go into a dry well or a bucket drain, and grease should not be poured down any drain in quantity. Wipe greasy pans with a paper towel and dispose of the grease in the trash before washing. This protects the drain, the dry well, and the septic or sewer system alike.

**How often should I clean an outdoor sink?** Rinse and wipe it after each use, deep-clean it with warm soapy water weekly during heavy use, and check the trap, seals, and faucet for leaks monthly. In a coastal location, rinse salt spray off the sink and faucet regularly and dry the basin to slow corrosion.

An outdoor kitchen sink is a small appliance with outsized consequences. Choose the material and size for how you actually cook, pick drainage by what your local rules allow and what you will put down the drain, insist on a shutoff and a drain-down path if winters freeze, and place the sink where the prep work happens. Do that, and the sink becomes the quiet workhorse of the kitchen — the thing you reach for without thinking, every time you cook outside.
