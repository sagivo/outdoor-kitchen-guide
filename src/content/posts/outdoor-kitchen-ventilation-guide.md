Ventilation is the least glamorous line item in an outdoor kitchen build — and the one most likely to cause expensive regret if you skip it. A grill under a covered patio without a vent hood will stain the ceiling, choke your guests with smoke, and may violate your local fire code. Here's the complete rulebook.

## The one rule that decides everything

**If your grill sits under any overhead structure — a pavilion, porch roof, lanai, pergola with a solid cover, or pool house — you need a vent hood.** If the grill is in fully open air with nothing overhead, you almost certainly don't.

That distinction comes from physics and code alike. In open air, the thermal plume from the grill rises and dissipates. Under a roof, that same plume hits the ceiling: heat accumulates, grease vapor condenses on the surface (creating a flammable film over time), and smoke hangs in the seating area instead of drifting away. The National Kitchen & Bath Association notes that more than half of outdoor kitchen projects now include some type of overhead structure — which means most builds need to plan for ventilation.

Beyond comfort, there are legal reasons. Many local building and fire codes **require** a vent hood over a grill installed beneath a combustible structure. This isn't a suggestion — it's the kind of thing an inspector flags and an insurance adjuster asks about after an incident. Check your local requirements during planning, not after the stonework is done.

## What a vent hood actually does outdoors

Same job as indoors, harder conditions. A powerful fan pulls the rising plume — smoke, heat, grease vapor, cooking odors — into the hood and exhausts it outside the structure through ducting. That keeps smoke out of your guests' faces, prevents greasy residue from discolorating the ceiling and damaging outdoor furniture fabrics, and moves the intense heat of a 60,000+ BTU grill away from combustible framing.

Outdoor hoods work harder than indoor ones for two reasons: wind disrupts the capture zone, and the open sides of a pavilion let the plume wander. That's why outdoor hoods are sized more aggressively (more on CFM below) and why placement matters so much.

## Sizing: width, CFM, and mounting height

**Width.** The hood should extend at least **6 inches beyond the grill on each side**. A 30-inch grill head wants a 36-inch hood; a 32-inch head (like the [Napoleon Prestige 500](/grills/napoleon-prestige-500-built-in/) or [Blaze Premium LTE 32](/grills/blaze-premium-lte-32/)) also takes a 36-inch hood; a 42-inch grill needs a 48-inch hood. If you're running a grill plus a side burner or power burner under one hood, size for the total cooking footprint. An undersized hood is the single most common ventilation mistake — it looks fine and captures nothing.

**CFM.** Indoor rules of thumb (100 CFM per linear foot, or 1 CFM per 100 BTU) understate outdoor needs because of wind and open sides. For a typical 60,000–75,000 BTU built-in grill, plan on **1,000–1,500 CFM** of exhaust; high-output setups with multiple burners can justify more. Bigger isn't automatically better — excessive CFM without adequate makeup air can backdraft — but outdoors, erring high beats erring low.

**Mounting height.** Mount the hood **36 to 42 inches above the cooking surface**. Closer than 36 inches risks heat damage to the hood and makes the grill cramped to work at; higher than 42 inches and the capture efficiency falls off a cliff as the plume spreads.

**Ducting.** Use the duct size the hood manufacturer specifies — commonly 10 inches round for high-CFM outdoor hoods. Never neck down to a smaller duct: restricted airflow chokes the fan, grease accumulates in the constriction, and performance collapses. Keep duct runs as short and straight as possible, and terminate above the roofline, away from windows and seating.

**Filters.** Choose **baffle filters** over mesh for outdoor grills. Baffles force the airstream to change direction, flinging grease out of the air and into the filter tray — far more effective at the high heat and heavy smoke volumes a grill produces. They're also dishwasher-safe and essentially last forever.

## What to buy: outdoor-rated only

Not every range hood can live outside. An outdoor-rated hood uses 304 stainless construction (the whole body, not just the visible parts), a sealed motor protected from moisture, and components rated for the thermal load of open-flame grilling. Brands that specialize here include Blaze, Bull, Trade-Wind, Vent-a-Hood, Fire Magic, and Lynx — several of which match the grill brands in our [best built-in grills](/posts/best-built-in-gas-grills-2026/) roundup, so you can keep the whole island in one design family.

Expect to budget roughly $1,500–$4,000+ for the hood itself depending on width and CFM, plus ducting and installation. Fold it into the project budget early with our [outdoor kitchen cost breakdown](/posts/outdoor-kitchen-cost-breakdown/) and the [build cost estimator](/tools/build-cost-estimator/) — ventilation added as an afterthought always costs more.

## Don't forget the island itself

The vent hood handles the plume above the grill. The island needs its own ventilation too: enclosed grill cabinets require vent openings (top and bottom) so heat can escape and any leaked gas can't accumulate. This is especially critical with propane — the [natural gas vs propane](/posts/natural-gas-vs-propane-grill/) guide covers tank compartment venting in detail. Your grill manual specifies the minimum vent area; treat it as law, not guidance.

## Ventilation checklist for your build

1. **Overhead structure?** Yes → budget a hood. No → skip it.
2. **Check local code** before finalizing the design — some jurisdictions require hoods under any cover, others only under combustible ones.
3. **Size the hood** 6+ inches wider than the total cooking footprint.
4. **Spec 1,000–1,500 CFM** for a typical built-in grill; more for multi-burner setups.
5. **Mount 36–42 inches** above the grates; duct at full specified size, short and straight, terminated above the roofline.
6. **Buy outdoor-rated** with baffle filters — 304 stainless, sealed motor.
7. **Vent the island cabinet** per the grill manual, top and bottom.

Get this right and the hood disappears into the background — which is exactly what good ventilation should do. Get it wrong and every cookout smells like a campfire inside your pavilion.
